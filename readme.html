Summer heatwave explorer: README

Reference: S. C. Chapman and D. A. Stainforth (2026), 1960s European heatwaves are becoming summer climate, in review.

What this is

An interactive view of how the frequency and intensity of summer (June-July-August) heatwaves have changed across Europe since the 1950s, using the E-OBS gridded daily temperature data set. It uses the crossing-theory relation between the length of a hot spell, how often it is expected to occur, and the temperature threshold that defines it:

D(u) = 92 × RP(u) × (1 − C(u))

where u is a temperature threshold, D the mean duration of runs of days above u (days), RP the average return period of such a run (years), C(u) the cumulative distribution function (cdf) of daily summer temperature, and 92 the number of days in June–August. Given the cdf, any one of D, RP or u follows from the other two. The method follows Chapman, Watkins & Stainforth (2019), Warming trends in summer heatwaves, Geophysical Research Letters, 46, 1634–1640, doi:10.1029/2018GL081286.

Controls

VariableTX is the daily maximum temperature, TN the daily minimum temperature. Everything on the page is recomputed for the one you choose.
Window lengthThe cdf for a given year is estimated from the summers in a window of 9, 15 or 21 years centred on that year (“central year”). Longer windows give smoother, more reliable estimates but blur rapid change; shorter windows follow change more closely but are noisier. The first and last year of every window carry half weight, so that one extreme summer enters and leaves the estimate gradually, rather than switching on and off at once.
Return period(Regional time series tab.) A 10-year / 5-year button chooses whether the time-series panels are set around a ~10-year or a ~5-year return period: 10-year uses heatwave durations of 8–10 days, 5-year uses 4–5 days. The maps are not affected.

Tab 1: Maps (movie)

Move the slider, or press Play, to step through central years. Hover the pointer over the map to read the value at a grid cell. Save image stores the current frame as a PNG. Three maps are available:

Return period (dual duration scale)

The return period, in years, of a heatwave of a fixed duration, relative to a fixed threshold: at every grid cell, the 99th-percentile summer temperature of the baseline window (centred on 1960). It therefore shows how much more often a heatwave that was a 1-in-N-year event in 1960 now occurs. Small return period (red) means such heatwaves have become frequent; large (blue) means rare. Because the relation above is linear in D, one map serves two durations: the right-hand scale gives the return period for the longer duration (the colours are drawn from this one) and the left-hand scale gives the return period for the shorter duration, read at the same colour. The flat dark red at the bottom of the scale marks return periods of less than 1 year on the left-hand (shorter-duration) scale. White cells are sea, have insufficient data, or lie beyond the 99.5th percentile of the window’s distribution, where the estimate is not considered reliable.

99th-percentile temperature (threshold)

The temperature exceeded on 1% of summer days in the window centred on the year shown (the same map as Figure 1 of the underlying analysis).

Change in threshold since the baseline year

The difference between that threshold and the threshold in the baseline window (centred on 1960), in °C.

Tab 2: Regional time series

Click a square on the small map (or, on the Maps tab, double-click a location) to choose a region. Three panels follow the central year of the window; each vertical bar spans the range between two durations or return periods (blue and red dots mark its two ends):

  • (a) the return period of a heatwave of 8–10 days (or 4–5 days in the 5-year case) above the fixed 1960 threshold T0;
  • (b) the mean duration of heatwaves with return periods of 9–11 years (4–6 years), above the same fixed threshold;
  • (c) the threshold temperature T* that corresponds to a 10-year (5-year) return period for 8–10-day (4–5-day) heatwaves in each window’s own distribution. The horizontal line is T0; the black × marks each window’s own 99th percentile.

The grey shading combines three separate sources of uncertainty, each drawn around both ends of the bar:

  1. Bandwidth resolution limit (lightest): the cdf is smoothed with a kernel of fixed width, so temperatures cannot be resolved more finely than that width.
  2. E-OBS ensemble spread (middle): E-OBS provides an ensemble of equally plausible gridded fields; this band reflects its stated spread (the 5th–95th percentile range), averaged over the window.
  3. Bootstrap sampling uncertainty (darkest): whole days are resampled (keeping each day’s spatial pattern together, because neighbouring grid cells are not independent) to show how much the estimate would vary for a finite sample of days; the 5th–95th percentile range of 100 resamples is shown.

Regions

The map is divided into a fixed grid of regions of 5×5 grid cells (about 1.25° × 1.25°). Each region pools the daily values of its 25 grid cells, which reduces noise compared with a single cell. All numbers were calculated in advance with the same method, window weights, kernel bandwidth and 100-resample day-block bootstrap as the maps. Regions with too little data (mostly sea) are not selectable.

Method in brief

  • Data: E-OBS v33.0e ensemble-mean daily TX and TN at 0.25°, June–August, 1950–2025, over 10°W–20°E, 35°N–65°N.
  • Estimating the cdf: kernel density estimate with a Gaussian kernel. One bandwidth is used everywhere (maps, regions and bootstraps; TX and TN each have their own): Silverman’s rule applied to a random subsample (200,000 values) of all summer values in the domain.
  • Windows: centred on every year for which the whole window lies inside the record.
  • Return periods follow from the equation above; values for which the baseline threshold lies beyond the 99.5th percentile of a window’s cdf are masked.

Caveats

  • E-OBS is an interpolation of station observations; station coverage differs between regions and has changed over time (it was sparse in the early 1950s in several regions). Early-period values and their uncertainty should be read with that in mind.
  • Individual very hot summers can cause visible steps in the time series as they enter or leave a window (the half weighting on the first and last year only softens this). This is a property of estimating a rare-event tail from a short window, not of the data.
  • The 0.99 quantile and the return periods are estimated, not observed; see the uncertainty bands.
